The tall ones: almost certainly ginger family

One test settles it, and it takes two seconds

Your close-up photo shows a pseudostem — an upright green "stem" made of tightly rolled leaf sheaths wrapping each other — with leaves coming off in two flat ranks and a new leaf emerging rolled into a spear at the top. Aspidistra, aglaonema and dracaena all fail that description. Gingers match it exactly.

The test: tear a piece off the edge of an older leaf, crush it hard between your fingers, and smell. A warm spicy or gingery smell confirms Zingiberaceae. Then push a finger into the soil at the base — a fat horizontal rhizome rather than ordinary roots confirms it twice over.

Either way they are in the wrong place. Nothing on that shortlist is happy on a dim lower shelf. Both are set to the front row at the glass. If it turns out to be a ginger, expect to water it roughly twice as often as anything else you own.

The small broad-leaf one shows yellowing tips and brown spotting. In a monsoon month with low light that is most likely tap-water salt build-up plus too little light. Flush the pot through with plenty of water once, move it closer to the window, and stop wetting the leaves.

Identify a plant

Three questions, then a shortlist

Photographing for identification

  • The whole plant from the side, at its own height. Not from above — growth habit is half the identification.
  • One leaf flat against a plain surface, daylight, thumb beside it for scale.
  • The underside of that leaf. Colour, hairs and veins separate species that look identical from above.
  • Where the leaf meets the stem or soil. This is the shot that solved your tall ones. Everyone forgets it.
  • Any new growth, flower or offset. Often a different colour, and frequently the deciding detail.
Shoot by the window with the mesh slid open, never with flash. Flash flattens texture and shifts colour, and both matter.

The year, as this room feels it

Talcher is inland, so the year is sharper at both ends than the coast. May averages 45.3°C highs with the longest sunshine of the year; January nights average 8.4°C and have touched 4.5°C. August brings around 340 mm of rain. A true south aspect means the sun swings from useless in June, when it passes almost overhead and slightly north of you, to genuinely good in December, when it sits low in the southern sky and shines straight in through the glass.

Sharing an enclosed room with plants

  • Do all your potting and soil mixing outside this room. Dry cocopeat dust drifting onto fruiting bags is a direct contamination route, and in a closed room it has nowhere to go.
  • Never spray neem oil or any fungicide in here. Mushrooms are fungi. Carry the plant out, spray, dry, bring it back.
  • Keep bags off the tiles, on a tray on the lowest shelf. Splash-back from a wet floor carries bacteria onto the substrate.
  • Solid tray on the shelf directly above, so plant drips don't land on them.
  • Spores are the real cost of a closed room. Pink and golden oyster and reishi are heavy sporers. Harvest young, wear a mask when handling, air the room hard afterwards. If anyone in the house is asthmatic, grow grey oyster and nothing else.
  • Green powder on a bag is Trichoderma. Bag it, bin it outside, wash your hands. Do not cut it out.
  • Sour or ammonia smell means bacterial contamination. Discard.
  • Yellow sticky traps handle fungus gnats and are safe around both.

Getting spawn

  • KVK Angul is nearest and normally stocks oyster and paddy straw in season, often with free training days.
  • OUAT Bhubaneswar runs the mushroom research programme and sells spawn including milky — 150 km, phone before you drive.
  • Private trainers around Bhubaneswar run three-day courses covering paddy, oyster and milky for around ₹2,000, usually starting a second Saturday.
  • DMR Solan ships mother spawn by post for the species nobody stocks locally — wood ear, reishi, golden oyster.
  • Roughly ₹40 a kilo, or ₹40–80 a bottle. Buy fresh, use within 2–3 weeks, keep it cool and out of the sun on the way home.
  • Straw is nearly free around Talcher after harvest. Insist on dry, golden, un-mouldy.
Start with one grey oyster bag in October, not five. One bag teaches you what full colonisation looks like and what contamination smells like, for the price of one bottle of spawn.

Pot sizes

Diameter across the top · volume is soil actually needed
Pot up one size, never two. Under 6 inches go up an inch; above it, go up two. A plant moved into a pot far too large sits in soil its roots can't drain, and rots from the middle out. This is the single most common way beginners kill a healthy plant.
No gravel or crocks in the bottom. It's the most repeated bit of garden advice and it's wrong — a coarse layer under fine mix raises the waterlogged zone instead of draining it. Fill with mix, straight to the bottom, and make sure there's a hole.

Repot when roots come out of the drainage hole, water runs straight through without wetting anything, the pot dries out in a day, or growth has stopped despite good light. Best months for you: October to November, and February to March. Not May — a root-disturbed plant in a 45°C room is a dead plant. Not deep monsoon either, when cut roots rot before they heal.

Feeding

—
Read this before buying any fertiliser. In your room light is the limit, not food. A plant in dim light cannot use what you feed it, so the salts stay in the soil and burn the roots — you get brown leaf tips and slower growth, the exact opposite of what you wanted. The grow bar will do more for growth than every bottle on this page combined. Feed after the light, not instead of it.

The five rules

  • Water first, feed second. Fertiliser onto dry soil burns roots directly. Wet the pot, wait ten minutes, then feed.
  • Nothing for six weeks after potting. Magic Soil already carries about a month of food. Feeding on top of it is how people burn new roots.
  • Half what the label says. Indian NPK labels assume open ground in full sun. In a pot indoors, half strength is the ceiling and quarter is often better.
  • Never feed a sick plant. Wilting, yellowing, pests, sunburn — fix the cause. Food makes a struggling plant worse, not better.
  • Stop in November. Growth stops when nights hit 8–9°C. Anything you add in December just accumulates as salt.

Flushing — the bit nobody mentions

Talcher tap water is hard, and in a closed room every drop that evaporates leaves its salts behind. They build up for months, then show as brown crispy leaf tips and a white crust on the soil. Your small broad-leaf plant is showing exactly this.

Every two months: take the pot to the bathroom and run water slowly through it until roughly three times the pot's volume has drained out of the bottom. For a 4-inch pot that's about a litre and a half. Let it drain fully before it goes back on its saucer.

Do it the day before you feed, never after. If you ever collect rainwater in the monsoon, that is the best flush there is — and the only water carnivorous plants will tolerate at all.

Care

Written for this room, not for gardening in general
  • Slide the mesh open by day. Insect mesh passes roughly 60–70% of light and glass takes another slice, so you're getting about half of what an open window would give. Both panels slide — park them aside during the day, close them at dusk against mosquitoes. Costs nothing, biggest single gain available to you. Also, the protective film is still on your window frames. Peel it off.
  • Light falls off fast inside a room. A plant a metre back gets a small fraction of one pressed against the glass. That's why the zones here are measured in distance from the window, not shelf height.
  • May will be hotter inside than outside. A glazed room with limited airflow behaves like a greenhouse, and Talcher already reaches 45°C. Sealed on a May afternoon this room could pass 50°C and kill everything in a day. Windows open, fan running, shade net on the outside of the opening.
  • Coal dust is a light problem, not a cleaning problem. A grey film builds within days here, and in a room already short of light it's the difference between growing and merely surviving. Wipe broad leaves weekly, rinse the rack monthly. Never leaf-shine sprays — they trap dust worse.

The habits that matter

  • Test before watering. Push a bamboo skewer 3 cm in. Damp and dark means wait.
  • Every pot has an open drainage hole. Nursery pots often ship with them half-sealed by the mould.
  • Quarantine new arrivals two weeks, in a different room.
  • Water at dawn March to June, not evening.
  • Rotate pots a quarter turn weekly. Non-negotiable with one-directional light.
  • Check the room temperature on a hot afternoon before you trust it.

Eight plants, outdoors

The container orchard

Everything here lives in a bag on the ground, not in the room. Tap a plant for its bag ladder, its watering by season, the one disease that actually kills it, and the thing about it you would otherwise get wrong.

A small bag does not force top growth

Canopy size follows root volume, so restricting the root slows the upper part rather than speeding it. But an oversized bag is worse: unused wet mix goes anaerobic and rots the collar, which in laterite soil under 1400 mm of rain is the commonest way these plants die.

The rule: step up. Each new bag roughly 1.5–2× the root ball, or four to six inches more diameter. The exception is the papaya, which is a taproot species that establishes badly after disturbance and so gets exactly one move, straight into its final bag.

Rules that cut across all eight

Direct sow, never transplant

Radish, okra, bush bean, amaranth, roselle, microgreens. All have taproots that fork or stall if disturbed — they go straight into the final bag.

Tray, then transplant

Every tomato, chilli, capsicum, brinjal, marigold. Lettuce either way.

One plant per bag

For the fruiting crops only. Two plants in one bag halves the yield of each and roughly doubles the disease risk — they share a water column, so it swings wet-dry faster, and swinging moisture is what splits fruit and causes blossom end rot.

Depth versus width

Radish, carrot and jamun need depth and nothing else. Lettuce and amaranth need width and almost no depth. One bag size for everything wastes money in both directions.

Bury the tomato stem

Strip the lower leaves and bury two-thirds of the stem. It roots along its whole buried length, giving two to three times the root mass — the highest-return move available in a container. Never do this with the grafted brinjal or chilli: it buries the union and destroys the wilt resistance.

Root-prune every second year

Once a woody plant is in its final bag: lift it, shave two inches off the sides of the root ball, refresh the top third of the mix, return it to the same bag. This is what stops a permanent container plant stalling.

Five microclimates, no air conditioning

Put each plant where it already wants to be

A gradient of five real microclimates beats one cooled room, and it costs nothing to run. The rule throughout: plants stay put, you do not shuttle them. A shade-grown plant moved into 45 °C sun scalds in two to four hours and the bleached patches never recover.

Why not air conditioning

Light is the reason. Noon sun here is roughly 80,000–100,000 lux; a bright indoor room gives 500–2,000. That is one to two percent. Leaves survive on that, flowers do not — flower initiation and sugar accumulation are the first things a plant drops when light is short.

AC also holds humidity at 40–50% in dead-still air, which is ideal for spider mites, and it erases the seasonal cues plants use. Mango needs a cool dry spell to initiate flowering, custard apple needs to drop its leaves, fig needs a dormancy signal. A constant 24 °C means the plant never knows what month it is, and never flowers. This is why indoor citrus in cold countries is famously all leaf.

Build this instead. 50% shade cloth on a frame over Zone A, with a low-pressure fogger line on a timer. A few minutes of misting each hour through the afternoon drops air temperature 5–8 °C and lifts humidity to 60–70%, which is what a tropical fruit plant actually wants in May. Put the drip line on the same timer and the May watering problem disappears with it.

The coal-belt problem

Fly ash settles on foliage and blocks photosynthesis. Hose the leaves down weekly, early morning so they dry before evening. Ash deposition also pushes soil alkaline — test pH occasionally, and above 7.5 citrus and jaboticaba show iron chlorosis, yellow leaf with green veins. Correct with chelated iron and a little elemental sulphur.

Sowing tomatoes

One plant per bag, and never sow into the final bag
Seed-starting mix is not potting mix. It must be sterile, fine and deliberately low in nutrients — rich mix burns the radicle and invites damping off. No garden soil at all: it carries the Pythium and Rhizoctonia that topple seedlings at the soil line overnight, and there is no cure once it starts.
  • Depth 0.5 cm, about twice the seed's own width. Deeper and it exhausts itself before reaching light.
  • Two to three seeds per cell, thinned to one by snipping, never pulling.
  • Bottom-water by standing the tray in a shallow bath for ten minutes. Top-watering with a mug displaces the seed.
  • Strongest available light within 24 hours of sprouting, or they stretch leggy and never recover.
  • Cross-pollination does not ruin this season's fruit — it affects the seed inside, not the fruit you eat. But it does matter for the open-pollinated packet you are saving from: keep that plant three metres clear, or bag two or three trusses in muslin before they open.

Bags & mix

The terminology in the market is genuinely confusing — three different things all get called a grow bag, and only one of them is right for a place that hits 45 °C.

Which bag

Practical rule: fabric bags throughout, in a pale colour — dark fabric heats almost as badly as plastic. Fabric dries fast, which is the one real cost, and the answer to that is the drip line planned for Zone A. Until the drip runs, double-pot the small ones and mulch two inches deep.

A filled 24×24 bag weighs 120–150 kg. Fill it in its final position. Never fill and then move.

Three things people skip

Charging the coco peat

Raw coco carries sodium and locks up calcium. Soak the expanded block, squeeze and rinse twice in fresh water, then soak twelve hours in calcium nitrate at 10 g per 5 L, rinse once more, drain. Uncharged coco is a common and invisible cause of stunted, yellowing container plants.

Curing the mix

Five to seven days, moist, heaped, covered with a sack, in shade — before anything goes in. Trichoderma colonises and the neem cake stops being hot.

Drainage at the base

Two inches of broken brick or coarse gravel, and six to eight extra slits cut in the lower third of the bag. In August, drainage kills more container plants in this district than May heat does.

pH, because of the ash

Fly ash deposition pushes the mix alkaline. Test it. Above 7.5, work in 50 g of elemental sulphur per 100 L.

Fertiliser, start to finish

Feed

Most feeding mistakes are diagnosis mistakes. Start with the one rule that makes a yellow leaf readable, then the products, then the calendar.

The rule that solves most of it

Some nutrients move around inside a plant and some cannot. When nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium or magnesium run short, the plant strips them out of its oldest leaves to feed new growth — so the damage appears at the bottom. Iron, calcium, zinc, boron and sulphur cannot be moved once placed, so a shortage shows on the newest leaves while the old ones stay perfect.

So look at which leaves are affected before you look at the colour. Yellowing on old leaves and yellowing on new leaves are opposite diagnoses with opposite treatments. Treating one as the other is the single commonest fertiliser error, and it makes things worse rather than doing nothing.

What the numbers mean

N : P : K — nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium
Nitrogen builds leaves and stems. Phosphorus builds roots and starts flowers. Potassium fills and sweetens fruit. Almost every disappointing container fruit crop traces to one error: nitrogen carried on into the fruiting phase, which gives a large healthy plant carrying almost nothing.

Organic inputs

Slower, safer, and they feed the soil rather than the plant

When each wins. Organic inputs release slowly, improve structure, and are almost impossible to overdose — use them as the base of every mix and the routine top-dress. Synthetic solubles act within days and let you target a specific stage — use them for the fruiting switch, for correcting a diagnosed deficiency, and for feeding through a season when the plant is growing faster than compost can release.

There is no purity contest to win here. A vermicompost base with a fortnightly soluble feed during flush and a high-K switch at flowering outperforms either approach alone, and costs less than an all-synthetic regime.

pH decides whether any of it works

Fly-ash deposition here pushes soil alkaline
This is the part people skip and then blame the fertiliser. Above about 7.8 iron, manganese, zinc, boron and phosphorus are chemically locked into forms roots cannot take up. Adding more of them changes nothing. Test the mix with a ₹150 pH meter or a strip kit, and correct with 50 g of elemental sulphur per 100 L before you spend anything on feed.
